# CreatorsOnTheBlox - How to make successful Roblox games

Build a game with a distinctive format, world, or characters you enjoy, and check it with blunt collaborators before production. Scope the first release small enough to finish and finish well. Grow through lore, characters, collaborations, and off-platform IP rather than volume alone. Hire from your trusted circle, pay per deliverable, and fund projects sustainably. Run live ops as steady, scoped, reusable updates with buffers, and cancel what isn't ready. Caring about the work matters more than money alone.

**Style:** Honest, anecdote-driven guidance from a series of guest interviews with many Roblox developers, not one fixed playbook. Its strongest value is lessons from real launches, failures, scope overruns, and team management. Numeric growth tactics are sparse. Best for developers who want experience-based lessons on scope, team, and mindset.

## Playbook
1. **1. Pick a distinctive idea** [[game-ideas-and-genres]]: Choose a format or world nobody else makes, such as story films with music. Start from the build or aesthetic, then derive the mini-game. Pick settings by logic and stakes, for example a containment facility where threats cannot escape. Play widely across genres for inspiration.
2. **2. Maintain a large idea list and title it well** [[titles-descriptions]]: Keep a large running list of ideas (Dude993 cites about 5 pages of titles). Have trusted friends pick the most clickable title before scripting begins. Package titles and thumbnails so the video looks like a normal Roblox video before unfolding into the story.
3. **3. Validate before building** [[playtesting-and-feedback]]: Write the script or design first and read it aloud to the team. Only start production after it is green-lit. Ask collaborators for brutally honest, even biased, criticism. Playtest mini-games before committing, and cut generic loops that fail creatively.
4. **4. Scope to something beatable** [[development-process-and-speed]]: Make a small game you can finish in one sitting before a huge project. Zeal says jumping straight into a large scope made Pressure too big. Plan rework with reuse in mind so existing enemies and systems carry over. Avoid making a game too similar to a hit like Doors.
5. **5. Build in self-contained areas** [[core-loops-and-game-design]]: Build each new area as a floor with its own style and mini-game. Contained themes keep mixed styles cohesive and make updates feel fresh. Players finish floors in minutes, so plan reasons to revisit. Yeucc's Regretevator floors took about 80 seconds per visit, with a 3-4 person team.
6. **6. Craft art and animation deliberately** [[building-art-and-assets]]: Keep modeling topology clean, since messy sculpt topology hurt performance. Check references for AI-generated images before modeling. Give each character a distinct gait and motive rather than reusing idle or walk cycles. Roblox rigs lack animated faces, so show emotion through body movement and posture.
7. **7. Separate front and back ends** [[scripting-and-performance]]: Split front end (UI, animation, feel) from back end (generation, item logic, state). The back end feeds the front end via signals. If unfamiliar code breaks in playtest and cannot be fixed quickly, scrap it and rewrite from scratch, which can be cheaper.
8. **8. Hire people you trust and pay per deliverable** [[team-and-budget]]: Hire friends and warm referrals from other animators; cold outreach got overcharged or ignored. Pay well per deliverable, since money is the main incentive for most staff. For outside talent, a concrete fixed fee (Zeal cites $5,000) in a forum post works. Grow leadership gradually, from 3 people to 70+ over about two years.
9. **9. Teach players through the environment** [[first-session-onboarding]]: Players skip text and do not look things up. Place hints in the world, such as breadcrumbs or jingling keys, rather than relying on text prompts. Introduce new players with simple party games before showing complex ones like Doors.
10. **10. Set release windows with buffer** [[development-process-and-speed]]: Public release dates should include buffer for unforeseen events like a developer leaving or school. Piggy 64 Part 2 slipped about a year after its planned date because the scripting team turned over. Keep a buffer in the schedule and tell partners a project can take up to a year.
11. **11. Launch with early testers and strong icons** [[discovery-algorithm]]: Post game links in Roblox comment sections for a few instant testers from the front page. Replacing a weak game icon with a strong drawn one helped Copyright Artist get picked up by the algorithm with no ads. Shredd of Brain Rot was built within one week.
12. **12. Grow through characters, lore and collaborations** [[social-features-and-virality]]: Repurpose short clips into long YouTube videos; Wondernat's long-form TikTok avatar format took off quickly. Collaborations are usually just asking nicely by DM or Discord. Pitch a full competition bracket with prizes rather than single videos. Hide lore easter eggs, since players connect deeply with lore.
13. **13. Check ad settings and use off-platform IP** [[monetisation-other]]: Dude993 ran only two ads on a ~14-minute video for about eight months. He says the platform suggests 5-10 ads at that length, so audit your settings. Use Roblox revenue to fund shorts, music, and plush merch via Makeship, where you supply designs and they produce and sell.
14. **14. Run live ops as scoped, reusable updates** [[updates-live-ops]]: Vary each episode or update so audiences cannot predict it. Quality per update matters more than volume. Scope Roblox events to about one month, since Zeal says rushed events hurt. Cancel unready events rather than burn out the team; he shelved a 30%-complete Halloween event. Add daily runs and seasons for retention. Plan large reworks as separate overhauls rather than patches.

## Common mistakes
- Relying on AI auto-translation for game text; it overrode manual Spanish translations and mishandled nuance [XtWTnz56ZpM]
- Relying on AI moderation alone; automod missed contractions and evasion spellings like 'who are' [XtWTnz56ZpM]
- Building content you dislike; Zeal says burnout came from features he did not enjoy [Qn0uVUSxG8I]
- Making a game too similar to a hit like Doors; Pressure felt close to it and got boring two years later [Qn0uVUSxG8I]
- Accepting rushed event deadlines blindly; the Graveyard event was rushed and too ambitious [Qn0uVUSxG8I]
- Stopping updates of a successful game too early; The Sun Experiment's players left after abandonment [XtWTnz56ZpM]
- Setting fixed public release dates with no buffer; unforeseen events cause delays [WODeLtB2YH4]
- Ignoring ad settings; Dude993 ran only two ads on a ~14-minute video for months [WODeLtB2YH4]
- Funding multi-season events out of pocket; season 3 cost over $500,000 and lost money [W8ghgtipQgI]
- Assuming integrations stay; RB Battles lost multiple games and needed replacements [W8ghgtipQgI]

## Launch checklist
- [ ] Script or design is written and green-lit by the team
- [ ] Blunt feedback collected from trusted collaborators
- [ ] Mini-games playtested and weak loops cut
- [ ] First release scoped to be beatable in one sitting
- [ ] Release date includes buffer for team or school disruption
- [ ] Title picked from a larger list by trusted friends
- [ ] Thumbnail and icon are strong and specific to the game
- [ ] Ad settings checked against the platform's suggested count
- [ ] Early testers recruited via Roblox comment section links
- [ ] Onboarding hints placed in the environment, not only text
- [ ] Simple party game introduces new players before complex content
- [ ] Team hired from warm referrals and paid per deliverable
- [ ] Clean topology and distinct character motion checked
- [ ] Front end and back end separated with signals
- [ ] Event or update scoped to about one month of work
- [ ] Replayability plan for daily runs or seasons ready
- [ ] Off-platform merch or shorts considered to reduce platform dependence
- [ ] Funding model sustainable for repeat seasons, not a GoFundMe
